01 / About

My work did not begin in academia.

For around ten years, I worked with young people, civil society groups, dialogue initiatives and international organisations. Most of that work happened in and around the Mediterranean.

I come from the Levant, a part of the world where different identities, languages, communities and political realities live very close to one another. I became curious about people before I became curious about institutions.

I wanted to understand how people from very different backgrounds meet, disagree, organise and still create common spaces. That curiosity shaped my early work in youth participation, dialogue, diversity and civic life.

Later, another question became harder to ignore: who shapes the policies that affect those spaces, and how? That question slowly moved me from practice towards research.

The thread that still connects it all
People first. Then the institutions, rules and decisions that shape the space around them.
02 / Questions

What I am trying to understand now.

My current academic interest is European external action: how the EU acts beyond its borders, how different institutions and Member States work together, and how coherent that action is when politics change quickly.

I am especially interested in political transitions because they expose the machinery of policy-making. Decisions become faster, legal competences matter more, institutions negotiate their roles, and Member States may pull in different directions.

Syria brought me into this question through a region I know. Myanmar is helping me look at the same problem from somewhere I do not know in the same way. That comparison matters to me because I do not want my research to stay inside one geography simply because it is familiar.

Question one

Who gets to act?

How legal competences and institutional roles shape what the EU and its Member States can do.

Question two

Do they act together?

How coherence changes across institutions, instruments, public positions and policy areas.

Question three

Does context change the pattern?

What comparison across Syria, Myanmar and future cases can tell us about EU external action more broadly.

03 / Journey

Places changed the questions.

I did not follow one straight line from one university to another. I moved, worked, studied, travelled, changed plans and kept collecting questions.

My path took me from the Levant to Dubai, then through Bulgaria, Poland and Italy, with many shorter journeys in between. Poland is the first place in a while that feels like a base rather than simply the next stop.

Movement made me more interested in how institutions look from the outside and from the inside. It also made me less comfortable with easy categories. The same policy can mean something very different once you see the place where it lands.

04 / Research & work

Practice gave me the questions. Academia gave me new tools.

College of Europe

EU external action after political change in Syria

My thesis examined how the division of competences affected the coherence of EU reactions and actions after the fall of the Assad regime. It received the Prize for an Outstanding Master Thesis in European Neighbourhood Studies.

Current direction

Comparing Syria and Myanmar

I am exploring how legal, institutional and political organisation shapes coherence in EU external action during political transitions, using comparison to test whether the patterns travel beyond one region.

Before academia

Dialogue, participation and civil society

With Y-PEER, I led a national network of more than 700 members. At UNDP Syria, I worked on civil society policy and regulatory reform. My current work with MARA-Méditerranée focuses on advocacy, partnerships and regional cooperation.

Education

Jagiellonian · Siena · College of Europe

My academic path moved through international relations, public diplomacy, cultural diplomacy and European interdisciplinary studies. Earlier, I also completed a Bachelor of Dentistry at Damascus University.

05 / Elsewhere

There is a person behind the policy documents.

Travel is one of the ways I understand the world outside institutions.

I like arriving somewhere I do not know, walking without a perfect plan, noticing how a city moves, listening to languages around me, and seeing the small differences in everyday life.

I am drawn to photography, cities, languages and conversations with people whose backgrounds are different from mine. None of that sits outside my work. It is part of why I remain interested in borders, identity, institutions and the spaces people build between them.

For me, curiosity is not a professional slogan. It is the reason I keep changing the question.

Things I keep returning to
